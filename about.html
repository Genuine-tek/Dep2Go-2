Founded in 1994. Still Answering the Phone.

DEP started in 1994 as a small New Jersey operation with a simple premise: contractors lose more money waiting for parts than they ever save buying the cheapest one. Three decades later the premise has not changed, and neither has the scale. We are deliberately a family sized business — small enough that the person who takes your call is the person who works your search.

What has grown is the network. Every year in this trade adds suppliers, salvage yards, rebuilders and machine shops to the list of people who will pick up when we call. That list is the actual product. Anyone can look up a part number; the value is knowing who has the thing when the catalog says it no longer exists.

We are not a dealership and we are not an e commerce warehouse. We are the people you call when both of those have run out of answers.

What "Non O.E.M." Actually Means

O.E.M. means the part came through the manufacturer's supply chain. Non O.E.M. means it did not. It is a purchasing channel, not a quality grade — the bearing in an aftermarket box is often made in the same plant, to the same drawing. Where it genuinely differs is cost and availability, and both usually run in your favor:

Cost

Aftermarket construction parts routinely land well under dealer list for equivalent hardware — which matters most on the wear items you replace repeatedly.

Availability

When a factory backorder is measured in weeks, an aftermarket or good used part that ships today is not the lesser option. It is the only option that keeps the machine earning.

Longevity

Once a machine leaves the support window, non O.E.M. is the entire market. Used, refurbished and custom built components are what keep 25 year old iron on the job.

We will also tell you when O.E.M. is the right call. There are assemblies where the tolerance, the warranty, or the failure consequence justifies the premium, and pretending otherwise would cost you more than it saves. The full comparison is here.

The Network

A dealer can sell you what is in the system. When a machine ages out of support, the system stops returning results — that is where most parts searches stall, and where ours starts.

Thirty years buys relationships with the people who hold the inventory nobody catalogs: salvage yards parting out machines, rebuilders with cores on the shelf, distributors with dead stock, machinists who can make a legacy part from a sample. One obsolete search might touch a dozen of them in an afternoon. You place one call; we make the other twelve.
